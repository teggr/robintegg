I've just watched [Claire Vo's talk, The New PM, at Compile 26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4CAFK-rc26A), and the part that has stayed with me was how much access to AI she describes, and how much she spends on it. It got me thinking about token maxing: how people are pushing through as much model usage as possible, keeping agents busy and finding ways to get more capacity.

My feed is full of people using multiple Claude Max subscriptions, 10 agents at once, and producing hundreds of commits a day. I pay £40 a month for a personal Copilot subscription for hobby use. Even with the automatic discount, that doesn't stretch very far, so I imagine their usage costs add up quickly.

My next question is: what are they doing with all those tokens? If we're building software at this rate, where is it showing up in my day-to-day life?

## I'm seeing frequent updates, but I'm only using a fraction of them.

My own workflow has changed. I'm using the Copilot app and VS Code, trying a few extra tools and experimenting with agents. I've also started working across a wider set of projects. Those changes matter to me, but the software I rely on day to day hasn't expanded at anything like the rate of the announcements and token burn being touted.

For the software I am using, new features flash by in the wink of an eye. By the time I've noticed one, there's another release, another demo, another way to delegate work. I haven't even worked out whether the previous thing solves a problem I have.

Trying something properly takes time. I need to understand what it does, where it fits, what I can trust it with, and whether it's worth changing a habit. Watching a demo is quick. Absorbing a tool into my working day is a different commitment.

I don't have the capacity to see, understand and absorb all of it.

## What all that activity leaves behind

There are good reasons to use a lot of tokens. Exploring an unfamiliar codebase, trying something hard, testing different approaches, reviewing changes and throwing away a failed experiment can all be worthwhile. Some of the value will be in what someone learned rather than what they shipped.

I wonder if some token spending can become a kind of software engineering doomscrolling, with agents in place of a feed. It might mean generating a funny text animation, a 3D game, or another todo app just because it's quick to try. Those can be worthwhile experiments, but if they don't answer a question or become something someone uses, there's always another small thing to generate and move on from.

That can feel like a lot is happening while we're mostly consuming output, rather than making something we or someone else will keep using.

When most of the output is disposable, it competes for the attention we need to evaluate what ships and learn from it. More activity can leave less room to notice which ideas are worth keeping, so faster execution doesn't automatically give us more ideas worth building.

## Idea exhaustion sounds familiar

Claire's discussion of idea exhaustion connects with this. Her argument is that execution capacity can outpace conviction about what to build. The backlog can be cleared, the obvious competitor features can be copied, and teams can move on to the next release before learning much from the last one.

That feels familiar when I look at my feed. Another agent interface, another dashboard, another variation on a feature I've already seen. Some iterations will make an existing tool genuinely better, and that deserves credit. But I do find myself wondering who is thinking outside the box, and what problems we're now able to tackle that we previously couldn't.

Maybe my algorithm is poorly trained. If I keep watching coding-agent demos, I'll probably keep getting coding-agent demos. My feed is a narrow view of what's happening, so I shouldn't mistake it for evidence that everyone has run out of ideas.

Still, faster implementation gives us plenty of opportunity to repeat ourselves. Coming up with something worth trying seems to need its own time and attention, even when an agent can build the first version quickly.

## The part of the roadmap argument I buy

Claire argues that traditional feature roadmaps become dangerous when AI can accelerate poor judgement. I wouldn't assume engineering capacity has stopped being a constraint everywhere. Integration, security, review and maintenance still need work.

But I buy the concern about shipping faster than we can learn. As a user, I need time to discover a feature and decide whether it belongs in my workflow. A team that has already moved on may never find out why I didn't adopt it.

Her distinction between bets, experiments and promises seems useful here. An experiment can be discarded once it answers a question. A feature people depend on carries an ongoing commitment. Cheap implementation doesn't make that commitment disappear.

For my own use, I want a reason to spend more before I add more capacity. There's enough I haven't absorbed in the tools I already have.

I'd like to hear from someone using those 2 or 3 subscriptions: what have you built that you, or someone else, still use a month later?
